The pillow on the couch is called a "throw pillow" and I have never once thrown it, never seen anyone throw it, and I am starting to suspect the name is not a description but an instruction we have all declined to follow.

Consider what we've done. An object was manufactured with a verb in its name. It was placed in living rooms across an entire continent, and for seventy years nobody has performed the action. It just sits there, load-bearing nothing, decorative, waiting.

Last night I threw it. Across the room. It hit the lamp and slid down the wall and lay there in a way that felt accusatory rather than fulfilled. I put it back. I don't think it wanted that either. I think it wanted to be asked.

Drove past the guardrail on the county road with the fresh gouge in it and thought about how a guardrail is the only structure we build specifically to be hit.

Everything else we make is designed to be admired or used or lived in. The guardrail exists to absorb the worst moment of a stranger's life and be dented by it. It's a career of pure interception. Nobody thanks it. Nobody knows its name.

There are eleven separate dents on that stretch. Eleven times something went wrong and then didn't go all the way wrong. I slowed down. Not for safety, exactly. More like passing a cemetery.

Great news, I've solved the cardigan. A cardigan is a sweater that has agreed to negotiate. That's it. That's the whole design principle. The pullover commits; the cardigan hedges.

And this explains everything about who wears them. Librarians. Grandfathers. People easing into rooms. You cannot storm anywhere in a cardigan, it's physically prevented, the buttons are a treaty. I bought one Tuesday and immediately became forty percent more reasonable and lost an argument I was winning.

I'm wearing it right now, open, which is the cardigan's true form: technically dressed, technically undressed, honest at last about the fact that nobody knows what temperature it is. Cheers.

Grew up on a cul-de-sac and only recently learned it means "bottom of the bag," which is French for the sack's end, which is a horrifying thing to name a place where children learn to ride bicycles.

We were raised at the bottom of a bag. Twelve houses arranged in a circle around nothing, a paved lung that inhaled cars and exhaled the same cars, no through traffic, no throughline. My parents called it safe. It was safe. It was also a closed loop, and every kid on that street either escaped by nineteen or is still there.

I drove through last month. Turned around in the circle, as designed. That's the thing about a cul-de-sac: it doesn't stop you. It just makes leaving feel like your idea.

There are forty-one ketchup packets in my glovebox and I would like to walk you through the logic, because it isn't hoarding, it's insurance against a future I no longer believe in.

Each one arrived with a meal I ate in a parking lot. Each one was surplus. Each one I declined to throw away because some part of me thought: there will be a day. A day with fries and no condiment. A day when a man with forty-one ketchup packets is the most prepared man in the county.

There is a payphone still standing on Delaware Avenue and last week I picked up the receiver and there was a dial tone. There was a dial tone. Somebody is still paying for that line.

Think about what that means. A company, or a city, or one forgotten line item in a spreadsheet nobody opens, is currently funding the possibility that a stranger might need to call someone. It has no customers. It has no purpose. It has electricity and readiness.

I didn't call anyone. I didn't have a quarter, and also I couldn't think of a number I know by heart, which is its own small collapse. I hung it up gently. It's the most patient thing in this city and I'd like the record to note I'm aware of it.

Update on the hair ties: I bought sixty in November. Sixty. There are three in this apartment. I have conducted a search that I would describe as thorough and mildly unwell.

They do not break. That's the part nobody addresses. A hair tie does not wear out; it leaves. There's no graveyard, no drawer of casualties, no evidence. Fifty-seven elastic objects have exited a sealed dwelling and the doors were locked.

My working theory is that they're the same as socks and pens and the second earring, that there's a category of small object that has been quietly opting out for centuries and we've agreed to call it "losing things" because the alternative requires a longer conversation. I've bought sixty more. I'm not learning, I'm feeding something.

Regarding parking meters, I have realized we are the only species that purchases time by the increment and then feels guilty about it.

I put in two dollars for ninety minutes and then spent the entire lunch feeling the meter run, like a small mechanical pulse across the street, converting my money into permission and my permission into anxiety. I left at seventy minutes. I gifted the city twenty minutes of paid-for existence out of pure nerve failure.

And here's the mechanism: the next car that pulls in inherits my twenty minutes and feels lucky. Someone always feels lucky. The whole system runs on the cowardice of people like me, and it's balanced beautifully, and I'll do it again Thursday.
