The deck of cards in the junk drawer is missing the four of clubs and has been since roughly 2019, which means every game played in this apartment for five years has been played inside a lie we all agreed to.

I've watched guests shuffle it. Nobody notices. The absence does its work quietly, the way absences do, and somebody somewhere has been dealt a hand that was structurally incapable of being what it should have been.

I could buy a new deck for two dollars. I won't. The missing card is the only member of this household that got out.

Please be advised that the radiator in the north bedroom clanks in a pattern. I have transcribed it. Three short, pause, one long, pause, three short. That is not thermal expansion. That is somebody knocking.

I contacted building maintenance and was told it's "air in the pipes." Air. In the pipes. As though air enters a closed system on its own initiative and elects to communicate in a rhythm.

I have begun knocking back. So far nothing has changed, but I want the record to show that when it does change, I was the one who initiated contact, and I did so on behalf of the entire building without compensation.

So I got a loyalty card at the sandwich place and I've been thinking about the word "loyalty," which is a word we previously reserved for dogs and soldiers and now apparently applies to my relationship with turkey on rye.

Nine punches and the tenth is free. But consider: the sandwiches are not free. I have paid for ten sandwiches across nine transactions. What I have actually purchased is the sensation of being rewarded, which they have marked up considerably and which I would buy again.

I'm at seven punches. I'd walk through fire for that place. That's the whole design.

Margarine is a substance that exists entirely to be almost something else and I don't know why nobody talks about this. It sits in the fridge in a tub shaped like optimism. It spreads. It performs.

My grandmother kept margarine tubs after they were empty and filled them with soup, so that opening her freezer was an act of pure faith — every container lied about its contents and every meal was a surprise. I think about that constantly. She died with eleven tubs of unlabeled broth and we ate all of them and every single one was good.

I bought margarine yesterday for no reason. I don't cook. It's in there now, waiting to be a container.

Found a dandelion growing out of a crack in the loading dock concrete and I sat down next to it for eleven minutes, which is the longest I've sat with anything this year.

Everyone calls it a weed, which just means a plant that showed up without an invitation and refused to apologize. It cracked concrete. Concrete. Whole civilizations are built on the assumption that concrete holds, and this thing came up through it on a budget of rainwater and stubbornness.

I did not pick it. Picking it seemed like something a person would do to feel involved.

The streetlight outside my window has gone out and come back on four times tonight, always as I look at it, never when I look away, which raises questions about whether it's malfunctioning or whether it's shy.

I tested this. I stared for six minutes: steady. Turned to the wall: click, darkness. Turned back: on. This is either a coincidence with excellent timing or the light is doing something it does not want witnessed.

I have not reported it to the city. If I report it they'll send a man in a bucket truck and he'll replace the bulb and whatever was happening will stop happening, and I'll have traded a mystery for adequate lighting. That's not a trade. That's a surrender.

Confession: I still have the hall pass from Mrs. Danner's tenth-grade English class. Laminated. A wooden paddle with a hole drilled in it. It permitted me to exist in a corridor.

I think about the fact that there was a period of my life when moving through a building required documentation, and that I accepted this completely, and that at no point did I ask who issued the authority to Mrs. Danner. She had a paddle. That was enough.

Nobody has given me permission for anything since 2004 and I have never fully recovered from the transition. Some mornings I hold it.

Regarding the mildew situation in the bathroom, I would like to reframe the discussion. We have been treating this as a cleaning problem. It is a jurisdictional problem.

Mildew does not spread so much as it establishes. It selects a grout line, it commits, and it begins the slow work of proving that the wall was always going to belong to it. I have applied bleach three times. Three times it has returned in exactly the same configuration, which is not regrowth, that's reconstruction from blueprints.

I'm not going to fight it anymore this month. We have reached what diplomats call a frozen conflict and what I call Tuesday.

Found a mothball in the pocket of a coat I have owned for two years and I have no idea how it got there because I have never in my life purchased a mothball. I don't know where one buys them. I have never seen the aisle.

And yet here it is, small and white and smelling like every grandmother's closet simultaneously, a scent that is not a scent but a decade. I held it and briefly was eight years old and standing among hanging garment bags in the dark.

I've put it on the windowsill. It's not doing anything. It's just there, quietly deterring something I can't see, which is more than most of my safeguards accomplish.

Quick update on the goldfish: still alive, which continues to surprise us both.

The ATM in the lobby thanked me for my patience last night and I want to know what patience it observed. I stood there for forty seconds. That's not patience, that's just standing. It preemptively credited me with a virtue I did not display.

Then it asked if I wanted a receipt and I said no, and it printed one anyway, and I stood there holding a small paper record of a transaction I had explicitly declined to have documented.

The machine and I want different things and only one of us has a printer. I've filed the receipt. I don't know where else to put a thing like that.

I am currently wearing a lanyard for a conference that ended in October and I want to be honest that it is not an accident anymore.

My slippers have molded to my feet so precisely that they are now, technically, a record of me — a plaster cast made of fleece and repetition, and when I'm gone somebody will find them and know the exact way I put weight on my left heel.

That's the part that gets me. Not the death thing. The fact that the evidence of how I stood in a kitchen will outlast my ability to explain why I stood there so long.

They're falling apart. The sole on the right is separating. I've been told to replace them and I keep saying I will. New slippers wouldn't know anything about me. We'd have to start over.

Emergency: there is a rubber duck on the ledge of the building across the street, third floor, facing my window, and it was not there Sunday.

The meatloaf recipe has been in the family for four generations and I have finally read it closely enough to notice that it says "bake until done," which is not an instruction, that's a riddle.

Nobody in my family has ever asked what "done" means. We each inherited a private definition and we've been eating slightly different meals under the same name for a hundred years, all of us convinced we were participating in a tradition. There is no tradition. There is a piece of paper and eleven interpretations of it.

I made it Sunday. It was done. I decided it was done. That's apparently how this works and how it has always worked.

Something has happened to the keyboard and I need you to understand I am not being dramatic. The letter E is worn completely blank. Also A, S, and N. Everything else is legible.

That's not random wear. That's a portrait. Twenty years of me, rendered in absence, and it turns out I am primarily made of E. The Z is pristine. The Z has never been asked for anything. Some days I envy the Z and some days I think it must be terribly lonely over there.

Located a protractor in a drawer this morning and held it for a while, this half-moon of scratched plastic whose only job is to insist that angles are a real and measurable thing.

I have not needed to measure an angle since 1998. Not once. In twenty-six years of adult life, no situation has required me to determine that something is fifty-five degrees. And yet they gave one to every child in the country, at scale, in bulk, as though we were all about to be surveyors.

I think it was never about angles. I think they were teaching us that the world could be measured, and then we grew up and found out which parts couldn't, and the protractor stayed in the drawer to remind us we were told otherwise.

Waited at the crosswalk yesterday for two full minutes on an empty street at 6 a.m., no cars in either direction, and I did not cross, and I want to examine that.

The button does nothing. Everyone knows the button does nothing; it's a placebo installed to give the waiting a shape. But I pressed it, and then I honored the outcome of a ritual I know to be theatrical, on behalf of traffic that did not exist, witnessed by nobody.

Then the little white man appeared and I walked, and I felt permitted, and the feeling was genuine. I'd like to know what else in my life is running on the same mechanism, but honestly I don't want the list.
